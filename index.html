Plate I · Cartography

How everything is connected

The internet has the same architecture as the cosmos: almost everything is empty, and all the matter gathers along filaments.

There is no center of the universe, and no center of the internet — only gravity. Networks (about 78,000 autonomous systems) fall toward one another wherever it is cheapest to exchange traffic, and those meeting places become the superclusters: internet exchange points like DE-CIX Frankfurt, where peak traffic passes 17 Tbps. The role of gravity is played by a protocol, BGP, which does nothing but tell every network where every other network can be reached.

Between the clusters run the cosmic filaments: roughly 600 submarine cables, about 1.5 million km of glass on the seabed — enough to wrap the Earth 37 times. A single modern cable carries hundreds of terabits per second down fibers thinner than a human hair. Around 95% of intercontinental traffic goes under the ocean, not through space.

Plate II · Mass Survey

How much data exists

About 181 zettabytes — 181 followed by 21 zeros — and most of it, like most of the universe, is invisible.

The global datasphere — everything created, captured, copied and consumed — reached an estimated 181 zettabytes in 2025. Numbers that size stop meaning anything, so climb the ladder below: each rung is a thousand times the one before it.

The dark matter problem. Astronomers can only see ~5% of the universe; the rest is dark matter and dark energy. The web has the same ratio. Search engines index only the surface web — estimates put it at roughly 4–10% of all content. The rest is the deep web: your inbox, bank records, medical systems, paywalled archives, corporate databases — real mass, exerting real gravity, emitting no light.

Plate III · Expansion Survey

How fast it is expanding

The Big Bang was October 29, 1969 — one message, two computers, and it crashed after two letters.

At 10:30 pm, a student at UCLA tried to send LOGIN to a machine at Stanford. The system died after LO — and so the first word ever transmitted on the internet, fittingly, was “lo.” Four computers made up the whole universe that night. Today 5.6 billion people — 68% of humanity — are inside it, along with some 18 billion connected devices that outnumber us more than two to one.

And unlike the real universe, this one’s expansion is still accelerating: the datasphere roughly doubles every three years.
